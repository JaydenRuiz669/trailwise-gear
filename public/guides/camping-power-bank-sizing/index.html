How to size a power bank for a weekend camping trip

Estimate a weekend charging budget in watt-hours, compare ports, and test runtime before buying a larger battery.

By Trailwise Gear editorial desk · Published and source-checked October 26, 2026 · Desk research

In this guide

  2. Use energy rather than a large mAh number
  3. Build a conservative charging budget
  4. Check compatibility and usability

  6. Optional product research — BougeRV
The practical answer

List the devices and energy you expect to replace, allow for conversion losses and reserve, then test the full charging setup at home.

Make a device list

Write down every device you intend to charge: phone, light, camera, navigation unit, or tablet. For each, estimate how much battery you normally consume in a comparable day. Screen brightness, filming, temperature, and poor reception can change phone use substantially. Use your own measured pattern when possible.

Separate essential power from optional power. A phone used for navigation deserves a reserve that is not quietly spent on entertainment. Carry appropriate offline navigation and do not assume a battery bank makes a phone your only necessary backup.

Use energy rather than a large mAh number

Watt-hours are useful for comparing energy capacity. Goal Zero's calculator gives the relationship Wh = mAh × V ÷ 1000. Use the voltage associated with the stated capacity; do not automatically substitute a USB output voltage for an internal battery rating. If the manufacturer publishes watt-hours directly, start there.

Illustrative example: a hypothetical battery labeled 10,000 mAh at 3.7 V stores a nominal 37 Wh. That does not mean all 37 Wh will reach a device. Conversion, charging, and operating losses reduce usable energy. This example is arithmetic, not a runtime claim for a particular product.

Build a conservative charging budget

Suppose your planned recharging needs total 24 Wh. Dividing by a hypothetical 0.75 delivery efficiency produces 32 Wh of nominal capacity before reserve. The 75% figure is an explicit planning assumption, not a measured efficiency standard. Real performance can differ, so a home test matters more than a neat estimate.

Add a reserve appropriate to the trip and the consequences of running short. If you cannot estimate device demand reliably, simulate a weekend at home: start with a full bank, use the normal cables, and charge the devices you will bring. Record what the bank actually supports.

Check compatibility and usability

A bank can have enough stored energy but the wrong output for a device. Check port types, supported charging protocols, maximum output per port, shared output limits, and input charging time. Confirm that your actual cable supports the required charging mode.

For small lights or accessories, check whether the bank supports their low-current draw. Inspect the enclosure and connectors, use equipment from a traceable supplier, and follow the manufacturer's storage and temperature limits. Do not leave a battery in a dangerously hot vehicle or use one that is swollen or damaged.

Do you need a power station?

For USB devices on a short trip, a larger AC-equipped station may solve a problem you do not have. If you need AC power, a fridge, or repeated laptop charging, prepare a separate load and runtime calculation. Nameplate output watts describe power capability, not how many hours a battery will last.

Borrow before scaling up when possible. The best-value purchase is the smallest reliable system that meets your measured needs and reserve, with suitable ports and time to recharge before departure. We have not performed capacity tests on individual banks in this guide.
