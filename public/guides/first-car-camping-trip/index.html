Your first car camping trip: what to borrow, buy, and skip

A practical buying order for a first drive-up camping trip, with a packing checklist and ways to avoid unnecessary gear.

By Trailwise Gear editorial desk · Published and source-checked October 26, 2026 · Desk research

In this guide
  1. Start with the campsite, not the shopping cart
  2. Borrow first, then fill the gaps
  3. Pack by function
  4. Spend where failure would end the trip
  5. Before you leave
The practical answer

Borrow the bulky equipment, reuse your kitchen supplies, and buy only the items needed for the campsite and weather you have actually booked.

Start with the campsite, not the shopping cart

A drive-up campground and a remote hike-in site need different equipment. This guide is for a maintained campsite reached by road, with your vehicle nearby. Check the campground listing for drinking water, toilets, food-storage rules, check-in time, and whether a table or fire ring is provided. Confirm current restrictions directly with the land manager. A reservation does not guarantee that water is running or fires are permitted.

Choose a short first trip within a manageable drive of home. Arriving in daylight gives you time to pitch an unfamiliar tent and correct missing equipment. If the forecast exceeds your equipment or experience, change the trip rather than buying hurriedly.

Borrow first, then fill the gaps

Borrow a tent and suitable sleeping gear from someone who can show you how to use them. Set the tent up at home, check that poles and stakes are complete, and test your sleeping layout. A household blanket may be sufficient for some mild trips, but do not assume it replaces a temperature-appropriate sleep system. Ground insulation matters too.

Use your existing bowl, spoon, water bottle, towel, and durable clothes. Pack simple food you already know how to prepare. A no-cook menu can eliminate a stove purchase for the first outing. Keep perishable food properly chilled and follow local wildlife-storage requirements.

Pack by function

Shelter and sleep: suitable tent, stakes, ground protection if required, insulated pad, bedding, and dry sleep clothes.

Food and water: drinking water plus a contingency supply, meals, utensils, cooler when needed, and secure food storage.

Personal needs: medications, first aid, lighting with backup power, weather layers, sun protection, and hygiene supplies.

Trip logistics: reservation details, offline directions, a route shared with someone at home, rubbish bags, and required permits. The National Park Service's preparation checklist is a useful starting point; adjust it to the actual trip.

Spend where failure would end the trip

Our suggested priority is a dry and suitable shelter, a warm sleep system, reliable light, and enough water. A matching camp kitchen, roof rack, electric fridge, and elaborate furniture can wait. Keep a small budget for forgotten supplies and the drive home.

After the trip, write down three things: what went unused, what failed, and what would materially improve the next night. Borrow again when the need is still uncertain. Buy when the same problem recurs and a specific item solves it. That record is more useful than a generic must-have shopping list.

Before you leave

Pitch the tent once, switch the light on, check the pad for leaks, and confirm all required parts are in the vehicle. Agree on a weather cancellation threshold. Leave a route and return-time plan with someone you trust. The cheapest successful first trip is one that lets you learn comfortably without committing to a garage full of equipment.
